Supply chain traceability is essential for market access — forced labor, tariff compliance, deforestation, product passports, sustainability and certifications requirements all demand verifiable evidence for government authorities and customers.

Knowing you need traceability to address today’s challenges is only the beginning. Demands will continue to change quickly and sometimes unexpectedly. Today the focus may be tariffs, deforestation or product passports. Tomorrow there may be another law or regulation. Yet what is being required remains largely the same — the details of your supply chain, the origin of the inputs in your products, and the data and evidence to prove your claims.

We provide independent, practical guidance on the key considerations to be evaluated in designing an effective traceability program — and we help you determine what capabilities, including systems, solution providers and other strategies, will deliver what you need.

What this looks like in practice

Situations you may recognize.

Your Mexican-assembled furniture enters the U.S. at zero duty. Then Customs rules it is Chinese origin, and the duty jumps to 37.5%.

Based on what your supplier told you, you claimed USMCA origin for your furniture and paid no duty.

But some of the “Mexican” components were actually made in China. Once the true origin of the inputs is known, the furniture no longer qualifies under USMCA — and Customs determines that the assembly in Mexico did not change its origin. The furniture is therefore Chinese.

That means you ended up paying an additional 37.5% in duties on this shipment.

A supplier’s statement is not evidence. These modules detail the documentation to require at each tier — bills of materials, component-level origin records, supplier declarations backed by production records — so you are sure of your origin and tariff liability at the outset, rather than having to establish them after being challenged by Customs.

Your garment shipment is detained at the U.S. border. You need to prove where the cotton came from.

You know who manufactured the fabric, and you have the invoice to show it.

But that is not sufficient. You need the documented supply chain from the finished garments back to the cotton gin.

So you ask the fabric mill for the origin of the yarn and cotton in your fabric. The mill has to ask their yarn supplier, which may or may not be the yarn spinner, who then has to ask the cotton trader and so on. Some companies reply. Some send the wrong documents. Some go quiet. Meanwhile you are looking at the possibility of seeing the detained container re-exported.

These modules detail the information you need from each supplier and how to integrate and present it to U.S. regulatory authorities as a clear and defensible supply chain — which will allow the container to be released.

A software risk assessment platform flags a company for forced labor aluminum three tiers in the supply chain removed from you. The flag does not tell you whether the aluminum inputs from the flagged supplier are in your goods.

A risk alert from a software platform flags that one of your suppliers does business with a company that has been reported to source forced labor aluminum and that information is also known to U.S. Customs and Border Protection.

How do you respond to that risk? You are told that the flagged company in your supply chain buys the same aluminum input from six different sources. Only one of those six is on the list. The material in your order may have come from that one, or from any of the other five. The software thinks the company-to-company relationship exists, but has no granular information on whether the input from the flagged company was used in your shipment.

Finding out means going to the purchase orders and production records for your specific order, back to Tier 2 and Tier 3, and verifying the origin of the input actually used for your production. The software does not do that. But proving compliance with the law requires it.

Risk screening and documented evidence for your supply chain are two different products often sold with the same sentence. One without the other is telling you it might rain without providing an umbrella, let alone knowing whether when you try to unfurl it that it will work. The modules explain how to assess whether your traceability program is actually designed and integrated to protect your supply chain when the storm of government scrutiny finally appears.

Is This For You?

The first step isn’t buying a solution. It’s understanding what you need to know, show, and prove.

Companies can invest heavily in data, systems and solution providers and still find they cannot respond to the demands of government authorities, substantiate a claim, satisfy a customer requirement, or prove where an input came from.

You need traceability to respond to challenges from a growing list of market access obligations — and the scrutiny will continue to expand with the use of AI and more robust enforcement by government authorities.

The modules prepare you with a framework on how to respond not only to the traceability challenges on your agenda today, but also for the ones on the horizon that have not yet appeared — because traceability obligations demand the same answers to the same underlying questions and require the same evidentiary and data solutions.

Why we built this

We built TraceabilityTrends because we saw what happens when traceability is lacking or insufficient.

For years, we worked at the intersection of supply chains, trade, forced labor requirements and market access. We saw companies confront costly problems when they could not produce the right information, prove where inputs came from, or respond confidently when their supply chains were questioned by government authorities or even their own customers.

At the same time, we saw a growing gap.

As traceability became more of a focus, there was plenty of high-level information — and no shortage of vendors offering answers. What was harder to find was independent, practical guidance to help companies understand what they actually need before deciding what to build or buy.

That is why we created TraceabilityTrends.

We help companies understand the traceability landscape, ask the right questions and make better-informed decisions about their own programs.

We don’t sell traceability technology and we don’t promote particular solutions. We receive no referral fees or commissions from any vendor. Our only commitment is to offer practical and useful input on the fast-moving challenge of effective supply chain traceability.

The company

Why we built this

Between us, we have spent near a half-century advising companies on trade, labor, and sustainability issues across their supply chains. We have prepared scores of submissions to release goods from UFLPA detentions, with a 100 percent success rate. But that is fixing the problem after it has appeared — often at large cost to companies.

We want to help companies avoid a problem in the first place, and manage it quickly and economically should one arise — sparing the expensive delays and the costly legal and consulting fees.

To summarize: Prevention rather than cure.

We looked at what the market offered to help companies understand their own traceability compliance requirements, put the right program in place, and work out which elements could be handled internally and which outside services might suit them. What we found was too generic.

There are plenty of slide-deck presentations that explain traceability at a high level — but little that is practical enough to work through the questions a company actually has to answer. Too often the advice on offer ends in the same place: the product the vendor happens to be selling. That is understandable, but it does not help you decide what is right for your company.

So we did something different. We put that experience into four digestible modules covering the decisions every company faces before choosing systems, providers, or solutions. We are offering our best independent advice and not doing it as the basis to sell our clients other tools or programs.

Andrew Samet

Andrew Samet brings decades of experience navigating complex international trade challenges to TraceabilityTrends. His work has involved helping companies understand and respond to difficult questions involving trade, supply chains, forced labor and traceability — often where the stakes are high and the answers are not straightforward.

Throughout his career, Andrew has built a reputation for rigorous analysis, sound judgment and straightforward advice. At TraceabilityTrends, he brings that same approach to helping companies make informed decisions about traceability: cutting through complexity and market noise to understand what is required, what is fit for purpose, and where resources are best spent.

As an international trade lawyer and advisor, Andrew’s recent work has centered on compliance with the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA), traceability and origin requirements tied to tariffs imposed under the Trump Administration, and the expanding traceability rules emerging across the European Union and other jurisdictions. These requirements continue to broaden — extending beyond forced labor into environmental and other sustainability priorities.

Andrew previously served in senior roles in the United States Senate and the Executive Branch, and represented the U.S. government at the International Labor Organization. That background, combined with a career spent solving high-pressure problems for clients, shapes his approach: it’s far better to make traceability decisions before a crisis than during one. Through TraceabilityTrends, Andrew now makes that same crisis-tested learning widely available.

Andrew is a graduate of Yale University, earned his M.A. from Carleton University (Canada), and his J.D. from Georgetown University.

Akshay Sinha

Akshay Sinha co-founded TraceabilityTrends to help companies make sense of supply chain traceability — what the requirements actually demand, and what a workable program looks like for a business of their size and sector. This question is becoming harder to avoid as a wave of new regulations increasingly requires companies to prove where their inputs come from.

Akshay has more than sixteen years of experience advising governments, industry associations, and companies across consumer goods, agriculture, metals, and chemicals on the trade, labor, and sustainability rules reshaping global supply chains. Much of that work has turned on a point he returns to often: the pressure to trace a supply chain may come from tariffs one year and sustainability the next, but the underlying requirement — knowing your suppliers and holding the evidence to prove it — barely changes. Build once, and it serves both.

His work spans end-to-end chain-of-custody systems, supplier due diligence frameworks, documentation and data validation, and the integration of scientific, transactional, and operational evidence into origin, labor, and environmental programs. Working with clients across Asia, North and Central America, Europe, and the Middle East, he has secured approximately $15 million in duty savings through preference programs and rules of origin, and cleared nearly $5 million in UFLPA-related detentions before U.S. Customs and Border Protection — with a 100 percent success record.

Akshay holds a Master’s in Public Policy from Georgetown University, an MBA from XLRI Jamshedpur, and a Bachelor’s in Physics (Hons.) from Hindu College, University of Delhi.
